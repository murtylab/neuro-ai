Comparing Models and Brains

How can we tell whether an artificial neural network represents the world in a way that resembles the brain? These two interactive tutorials introduce two popular ways to make that comparison. RSA compares how stimuli are organized in the two systems. Encoding models ask whether model units can be used to predict neural responses.

A central question in this course is whether artificial neural networks are good models of the brain. But how do we actually make that comparison?

A brain gives us patterns of neural activity. Depending on how we measure it, those patterns might come from neurons, fMRI voxels, ECoG electrodes, EEG or MEG sensors, or something else entirely. A neural network gives us patterns of activity across model units. These are very different measurements, with no obvious one-to-one correspondence between them. Yet we want to know whether the two systems represent the same stimuli in similar ways.

There are several ways to answer this question, many of which we cover in the lectures. These two tutorials focus on two popular approaches: representational similarity analysis and encoding models. Each gives us a different way to compare representations in brains and models.

Each tutorial runs in the browser and includes interactive examples and a concept check. Work through them in order, since the second builds on the ideas introduced in the first.

Part 1

Representational Similarity Analysis

A representation is a shape, and a shape is fully described by the distances between the things it contains. That is what lets us compare a brain region and a network layer without ever matching a voxel to a unit.
Part 2

Encoding Models

Can a neuron be built out of a model's units? A weighted sum of model features is fitted to one neuron or voxel, then judged on images it has never seen, and read against the noise ceiling.
